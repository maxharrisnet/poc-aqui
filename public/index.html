What it costs to land.

A buying desk for importing vinyl into Mexico. It looks up each record on Discogs and adds shipping, duty and IVA to the listed price, so you see the real landed cost before you buy.

  • Type up to ten records: artist and title, then press Check these records. No list of your own? Press Use the demo list.
  • Read the price as one number. Every result is the total to get that record to your door, cheapest first.
  • Hover the coloured bar to see what each part of that total costs, or open a row for the full breakdown and a link to the listing.
  • Press the eye to watch a record. It joins the inventory as wanted; open the row to set a peso limit, and it tells you when a copy drops under it.

Stock on the shelf

Eight steps, about twenty seconds.

This demo does a small version of what the full system would do continuously in the background. Here's the whole chain, start to finish, so nothing about the numbers is a black box.

  1. You give it a list

    Here you type it. In the real system this is your Discogs want list, synced automatically, plus the artists and labels you follow, so you never type anything at all.

  2. It finds the release on Discogs

    Using the official Discogs API and nothing else. No scraping, ever: Discogs' terms prohibit it, and your account holds your inventory, so an enforcement action would hit the shop and not just the tool.

  3. It reads the current lowest price

    Live marketplace figures: what the cheapest listed copy costs right now, and how many copies are for sale. Both come straight from Discogs at the moment you press the button.

  4. It converts to pesos

    At today's European Central Bank reference rate. The real system also stores the Banxico FIX rate, which is what SAT expects when the purchase is later reconciled for accounting.

  5. It adds shipping

    Estimated from the pressing country. The full system replaces these estimates with real carrier quotes, then keeps sharpening them against what shipments actually cost once they arrive.

  6. It applies Mexican customs

    Records from the US and Canada under about USD $50 come in free under USMCA. Everything else picks up the roughly 19% tasa global, and then 16% IVA on top of value plus duty.

  7. It flags anything needing a broker

    Above roughly USD $250 a shipment needs formal customs entry through a licensed broker. That's a delay and a fee, not just a bigger number, so it gets called out rather than buried in a total.

  8. You get one number, and a link to buy

    A single peso figure per record, sorted cheapest first, with the cost broken into its parts and a direct link to the Discogs marketplace. You decide; nothing is ever bought automatically.

What's real here, and what isn't

Worth being precise about, because two of these numbers are solid and two are placeholders standing in for work that belongs in the real build.

Real, live data

  • Discogs prices and stock: pulled live from the official API each time you press the button.
  • Release metadata: artist, label, catalogue number, format, year, cover art, all from Discogs.
  • Exchange rates: today's ECB reference rate, fetched at the start of every run.
  • Customs arithmetic. The de minimis, tasa global, IVA and formal-entry logic all genuinely run.

Placeholder, pending real work

  • Shipping costs: sensible estimates by country, not live carrier quotes. Every one is labelled low confidence.
  • The customs figures themselves: from public reporting, not yet confirmed by a licensed broker.
  • Shipping origin. We use the pressing country, because the official API won't tell us where the seller actually is.
  • Matching. A search takes Discogs' best guess. Reissues and repressings aren't reconciled against each other.

Read before trusting these numbers

Five things that would change a total, and that this demo cannot yet get right.

  • Customs rules are not broker-validated. Duty, IVA and the formal-entry threshold come from public reporting on Mexico's 2026 Customs Law, not from a licensed broker.
  • Origin is the pressing country, not the seller's location. Customs depends on where a parcel actually ships from, and the official Discogs API doesn't expose that. A German pressing may well be sold from Guadalajara.
  • Shipping is illustrative, not a live carrier quote. The real system refines these from actual purchase data over time.
  • This is one snapshot, not continuous monitoring, and it takes the single cheapest copy listed, not the best copy at a fair price.
  • No identity resolution yet: reissues, repressings and regional variants aren't matched against each other.

The whole system 🗺️

A loop, not a line. Hover or tab to any box for what it is, why it's there and what it runs on. 💡

  • Sales pull stock down. Low stock switches a record's watch back on by itself.
  • Watched records drive the sources. Four places to find a copy, two to confirm which record it is, one engine to price it in pesos.
  • A person buys. Nothing here spends money by itself. The parcel that lands becomes stock, and the loop closes.
✅ Built & running 🔨 Known how, not built ❓ Still unproven 🧍 Human step, on purpose
  • ❓ Two boxes are still unproven. Matching: is this eBay listing the same record as that Discogs pressing? Barcodes help, free text doesn't. And intake: a parcel lands weeks after the buy and has to become a stock row without anyone retyping it.
  • 🧍 One step is manual on purpose. The system shortlists and prices, then stops. Ian reads the seller, the condition and the photos, and buys or doesn't. That judgement is what makes the shortlist worth having.
  • ⏱ Nothing waits for a button. Today a record is priced when someone presses Check listings now on its row. In the finished system a scheduled sweep does that for every watched record a few times a day, and the only thing that reaches a person is a copy under its alert price. The button stays, because whoever is at the counter should not have to wait for the next run.
  • 📋 One table, in a sheet for now. Stock and watches are one row per title: a record the shop wants is a row with no copies yet, and a sweep prices every row whose watch is on. It lives in Google Sheets because that's the tool Ian already works in, and a row is faster to fix than any form. Stock the shop floor depends on will need a real database eventually, with the locking, history and constraints a sheet can't offer.

Where the records come from 🌍

Seven sources, and none of them answers the whole question on its own. Four are must-haves, three are optional, and a few real markets are out of reach. Every API here is free; the cost is wiring time, not licence fees.

Answers it Partly Not available

⚠️ What the grid is telling you

  • The sources that find copies can't identify them. eBay and Mercado Libre fill every column but the last; their listings are free text. That last column is the whole matching problem, drawn.
  • The sources that identify records can't find them. MusicBrainz and Spotify fill only that column, which is exactly why they earn a place.
  • Discogs is a price without a seller. The deepest catalogue, and the only one that answers "what is this worth", but never "who has one".

✅ Must-have · build these first

🛒 eBay Browse API

The biggest upgrade · 🔨 not built yet
  • What it gives: real listings: who is selling, where it ships from, quoted shipping to Mexico, condition.
  • Why it matters: turns two of the cost engine's estimates, shipping and origin, into facts.
  • One call covers Europe: Germany, the UK, France, Italy and the Netherlands are a parameter on the same call. Sixteen marketplaces, one integration.
  • Limits: listings carry no release identifier, so joining them to Discogs is matching work, not a lookup; 5,000 calls a day on the standard tier; shipping to Mexico is only quoted if asked for.
Free · developer account, production keys

🇲🇽 Mercado Libre

No import problem at all · 🔨 not built yet
  • What it gives: domestic Mexican listings with price, seller and shipping.
  • Why it matters: no customs, no duty, no tasa global, no international lane. Landed cost is price plus domestic postage: the one source where our number is exact.
  • Limits: catalogue search needs only our own app token, but it's the same free-text matching problem as eBay; stock comes back as a coarse band, not an exact count.
Free · our own app token

📀 Discogs API

Already running · ✅ built
  • What it gives: the deepest catalogue in the business; lowest price and stock count per pressing, live.
  • Why it matters: the reference for what a pressing is worth. The number the whole engine runs on.
  • Limits: no marketplace listings, so no condition, seller or ships-from (origin is guessed from where it was pressed); 60 requests a minute, two per record, so sweeps are paced; scraping is out under their terms, and the account at risk is the shop's.
Free · personal access token

🧬 MusicBrainz

Cheapest, highest leverage · 🔨 not built yet
  • What it gives: barcodes, release groups and how pressings relate to each other, curated by people who care about exactly the distinctions a buyer cares about.
  • Why it matters: it finds nothing and prices nothing, but makes every other source match reliably. Goes in first, not last.
  • Limits: free, open, no API key; one request a second and a real user-agent is the whole contract.
Free · no account needed

🔨 Optional · worth having if the effort is there

📧 Bandcamp · via mailbox

Leads, never prices
  • What it gives: a dedicated mailbox receives follow notifications, so we learn a record was listed: a link and a date.
  • Why it matters: the indie pressings that never reach Discogs, from labels Ian already follows.
  • Limits: no catalogue API; the developer API is seller-side only (account, sales reports, merch orders); no prices in the mails, so never a landed cost; labels can't be followed programmatically, and coverage is the follow list.
Free · a fan account · rides the Gmail API

🏬 European specialist shops

One newsletter parser per shop
  • What it gives: new-arrival newsletters from HHV, Hard Wax, Decks and the rest.
  • Why it matters: they ride the same mailbox reader as Bandcamp. It's a shop-notification pipeline that Bandcamp happens to use first.
  • Limits: bespoke platforms with no APIs or feeds. We tested nine for the public Shopify product feed and RSS; none had either.
Free · newsletter sign-ups

🎧 Spotify

Identity only
  • What it gives: canonical artist and album names plus barcodes, and a way to seed the watchlist from Ian's saved albums instead of typing them in.
  • Why it matters: a second opinion on which record is which.
  • Limits: no vinyl, no prices, no stock; the artist merch is a Shopify storefront the API doesn't expose; five users in development mode, so never customer-facing; barcodes are usually the digital release's, a hint not an identity; recommendations were withdrawn for new apps in November 2024.
Free · developer account

🚫 Not available · real supply we can't reach

  • 🇯🇵 Japan. Yahoo! Auctions is where rare Japanese pressings trade; access for non-Japanese entities is restrictive and the proxy services everyone uses publish no APIs. Rakuten's item search excludes auction and private-seller listings: precisely the inventory worth having.
  • 📸 Instagram. A great deal of dealing happens there and effectively none of it is readable. The platform reaches only professional accounts your app user owns or manages; hashtag search is capped at 30 hashtags a week and approval-gated. The shops behind those accounts are the way in, not the feed.
  • 🏪 Shops with no website. Walk-in dealers, fairs and WhatsApp lists. There is no feed to read, so they stay a person's job.
Recorded, not planned
